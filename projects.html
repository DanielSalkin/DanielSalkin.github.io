Dash PCB

Power and communication
for the driver’s display.

The PLEX driver display needed two things the rest of our car could not provide directly: a 12 V supply and a Classical CAN (CAN 2.0) connection. Our telemetry network uses CAN FD, so the display could not simply be connected to that bus.

The Dash PCB supplies the PLEX with 12 V and acts as a CAN gateway between the CAN FD telemetry bus and the display’s CAN 2.0 bus. It also interfaces with the auxiliary switches for car on, cooling on, TV, TC, and regenerative braking.

Power, a CAN gateway, and cockpit controls

Bringing the PLEX into the vehicle network

The board bridges a power and communication mismatch. It provides the display’s required 12 V rail, which was not otherwise available in the car, and relays communication between the CAN FD telemetry network and the PLEX’s separate CAN 2.0 connection.

I led the hardware bring-up of the display’s power and communication interfaces, connecting the dashboard electronics with the vehicle network and driver controls.

Adapting to the steering-angle sensor

Later in the year, we adapted the Dash PCB to supply power to the steering-angle sensor and connect it to a private CAN bus. The sensor was internally double-terminated, a detail absent from its datasheet.

Giving it a dedicated bus kept that unexpected termination from affecting the shared vehicle network. The change made the dashboard an integration point for both the driver display and steering-angle sensing.

System architecture

The system diagram shows how the Dash PCB connects the driver display, vehicle control unit, steering-angle sensor, auxiliary switches, indicator LEDs, and power distribution board. Separate CAN connections let the board serve the display and sensor without imposing their interface requirements on the telemetry bus.

Generating the required power rails

The power sheet shows two buck-converter stages taking the vehicle’s 24 V supply down to 12 V and 5 V. The 12 V rail powers the PLEX, while a separate regulator derives 3.3 V from 5 V for the control electronics. Feedback networks set the converter outputs, with local decoupling and indicator LEDs supporting bring-up.

Keeping the CAN networks separate

The STM32G484 connects to three CAN transceiver channels. Those separate physical interfaces support the gateway between the telemetry and PLEX buses, along with the private sensor bus. The transceiver sheet also shows termination, transient protection, and common-mode chokes at the bus connections.

Reading the auxiliary controls

Five switch inputs carry the driver’s car-on, cooling, TV, TC, and regen selections into the board. The switch sheet defines each input’s high and low connections and resistor network, giving the microcontroller a defined electrical interface for the cockpit controls.

Coordinating the board

The microcontroller sheet brings the CAN channels, five switch signals, and fault inputs together. It also exposes programming and debug connections, making the controller accessible during hardware bring-up and firmware integration.

Integration

The design was constrained by cockpit packaging and controls integration requirements. Working with the drivers and ergonomics engineers, I redesigned and re-routed the board to fit the dashboard assembly, accommodate the driver controls, and meet the available space constraints.

The redesign reduced the board footprint by 23% compared with version 1.0 while retaining the power, communication, and switch interfaces the car needed.

02 / FORMULA SAE2026

CAN signal integrity

Better communication
starts at the physical layer.

I investigated how node capacitance, filtering, and bus topology affect communication reliability on our race car’s CAN and isoSPI networks.

Comparing scope captures before and after filter changes connected the circuit analysis to the behavior of the actual bus.

Why filtering became a signal-integrity problem

The investigation started with a tradeoff: the RC filters intended to reject noise also loaded the differential bus. I modeled the complete node rather than treating the transceiver as the only load, including protection devices, PCB traces, connectors, and the wire stub. More capacitance slows transitions and changes the impedance seen by a traveling edge.

CAN receivers measure the differential voltage, \(V_{\mathrm{diff}}=V_{\mathrm{CANH}}-V_{\mathrm{CANL}}\). Capacitive loading has impedance \(Z_C=\frac{1}{j2\pi fC}\), so its effect depends on frequency.

Differential signaling rejects noise coupled similarly onto both wires. It does not eliminate the effects of unequal loading, reflections, or slow edges. Because capacitor impedance falls as frequency rises, edge behavior matters as well as the configured bit rate.

Building the capacitance budget

The first-order capacitance budget is \(C_{\mathrm{node}}\approx\sum_i C_i\), summing the transceiver, TVS protection, filter, trace, connector, and stub contributions.

My PDB CAN2 lumped-load estimate used 10 pF for the transceiver, 8.5 pF for TVS protection, about 3 pF for PCB traces, 1 pF for the connector, and 15 pF for a 0.3 m stub. With the filter contribution included, the report estimated a 317.5 pF differential node load. Common-mode choke parasitic capacitance was treated as negligible in this first-order budget.

The report also explored a frequency-dependent impedance model at 17.85 MHz. That is a separate model from the summed capacitance budget; its effective capacitances should not be substituted directly into the lumped-load results without reconciling the assumptions.

From loading to reflections

The unloaded cable impedance is \(Z_0=\sqrt{L^{\prime}/C^{\prime}}\). Adding evenly distributed node capacitance gives \(Z_{\mathrm{loaded}}\approx\sqrt{\frac{L^{\prime}}{C^{\prime}+C_{\mathrm{node}}/d}}\), with reflection coefficient \(\Gamma=\frac{Z_{\mathrm{loaded}}-Z_0}{Z_{\mathrm{loaded}}+Z_0}\).

Here L′ and C′ are cable inductance and capacitance per unit length, and d is node spacing. Added node loading lowers local impedance, producing a negative reflection coefficient at the transition into the loaded section.

Applying the approximation \(Z_{\mathrm{loaded}}>0.71Z_0\) gives the spacing relationship \(d>\frac{C_{\mathrm{node}}}{0.98C^{\prime}}\).

Using the report’s 317.5 pF estimate and a cable capacitance of 50 pF/m gives d > 6.48 m in this distributed-loading approximation. That impractical result motivated reducing node capacitance rather than trying to solve the problem through spacing alone.

Termination and timing

I recommended short stubs, reducing RC loading, and split termination at the bus ends. Two 60 Ω resistors preserve 120 Ω differential termination while a midpoint capacitor provides a path for common-mode noise. A 0.3 m stub target is a 1 Mbit/s guideline, not a universal limit for every CAN FD rate.

Split termination preserves \(R_{\mathrm{termination}}=60\,\Omega+60\,\Omega=120\,\Omega\). The bit period is \(T_{\mathrm{bit}}=1/r_{\mathrm{bit}}\); during the \(4\,\mathrm{Mbit/s}\) CAN FD data phase, that leaves \(T_{\mathrm{bit}}=250\,\mathrm{ns}\) per data bit.

The practical question is whether propagation, edge settling, and reflections leave adequate margin before the configured sample point. Shorter stubs reduce the delay of returning reflections; simply selecting a later sample point does not fix excessive bus loading.

Extending the investigation to isoSPI

The isoSPI analysis compared multidrop wiring with a daisy chain. Daisy chaining removes the unterminated branches of the multidrop arrangement, but each device introduces forwarding delay.

For twelve devices at a forwarding delay of \(300\,\mathrm{ns}\) each, the return-path estimate is \(t_{\mathrm{round\mbox{-}trip}}=2\times12\times300\,\mathrm{ns}=7.2\,\mu\mathrm{s}\). Its reciprocal is \(1/t_{\mathrm{round\mbox{-}trip}}\approx139\,\mathrm{kHz}\).

This is the report’s simplified delay-only clock bound, before additional timing margins. It supported investigating a daisy chain for our 10 Hz measurement cycle; the sample rate alone does not establish a complete SPI timing budget. A second interface at the far end was proposed to provide a return path if one connection fails.

Outputs: a resilient 4 Mbit/s telemetry bus

The vehicle now has a resilient CAN FD telemetry bus operating at 4 Mbit/s. The study connected the behavior of individual interface circuits to the reliability of the complete on-car network, treating node loading, filtering, termination, and harness topology as parts of the same problem.

The reusable transceiver block carries that work into subsequent board designs. It gives the team a consistent interface to build from, so the findings apply across the car rather than remaining a one-board fix.

The filtering and loading trade study informed the CAN transceiver reuse block I created. This schematic is an output of the investigation: it captures the resulting interface design for reuse across the team’s boards.

Analog Swerve

Motion control,
built from the circuit up.

A proof-of-concept swerve module that turns electrical inputs into independent drive and steering control using analog circuitry and digital logic.

Swerve steering separates the direction a wheel points from the speed at which it turns. I explored whether a low-cost module could demonstrate those two controls with discrete electronics, without a software controller.

How the control loop works

Drive control

An R-2R ladder DAC converts the decoded digital command to an analog control voltage. That voltage drives a 555 timer to change the PWM duty cycle sent to the DC motor. Separate direction and enable signals complete the drive interface.

This split let me test each function independently: first the waveform, then the motor’s response, and finally its behavior once mounted in the module.

Steering control

A divided clock starts each servo command, while a separate counting path determines when the pulse ends. Varying that pulse width changes the steering angle without changing the frame timing.

Separating frame timing from pulse width let me test the PWM control unit one function at a time before connecting the servo.

Tracking the input

The input stage uses a tracking ADC to turn an analog command into a digital value for the control logic. An up/down counter feeds a ladder DAC, producing an analog reference that a comparator checks against the input. If the reference is too low, the counter increments; if it is too high, it decrements. The counter’s value becomes the digital representation of the input.

This forms a feedback loop that continually adjusts its previous estimate rather than restarting each conversion. Near a steady input, the output can alternate between adjacent codes; with a changing input, the DAC output follows in discrete steps. The scope capture shows that staircase response alongside the signal it is tracking.

The useful tradeoff is between resolution and tracking speed. Smaller DAC steps give finer voltage resolution, but a large input change requires more clock cycles to catch up. I used the oscilloscope to compare the reference and input directly, checking how well the circuit followed transitions before integrating it with the motor-control logic.

Validation & integration

I validated circuit behavior in LTSpice and with an oscilloscope before integrating the drive motor and steering servo into a 3D-printed assembly. The final build combines the mechanical module with breadboard electronics and physical input controls.

The project progressed through variable-duty-cycle generation, full drive control, servo timing, mechanical integration, and integrated controls. The prototype demonstrated independent drive and steering control; the final report records the completed milestones and areas for improvement.

04 / FABRICATION & ROBOTICS2024

Voron Printer

Building the tools
to build the next thing.

I fabricated a custom Voron Micron+ using commercially available parts and 3D-printed components, bringing the mechanical assembly, electronics, and wiring together into a working printer.

The build went beyond assembly: I implemented CAN communication to simplify harnessing and reduce the impact of EMI, then tuned motion and temperature control. The photos here show a separate Voron V0 I built for my robotics team.

Building and tuning the Micron+

Mechanical and electrical integration

The printer combines sourced hardware with printed structural and mounting components. Integrating those parts meant treating the motion system, electronics, and harness as one assembly, with wiring that could accommodate the machine’s moving parts.

CAN communication simplified the harness, reduced the impact of electromagnetic interference, and supported interoperability with similar printers. It also connected this build with the communication and wiring work I enjoy in vehicle electronics.

Motion and thermal control

I used input shaping to mitigate mechanical resonance during motion. That tuning addresses the machine’s dynamic behavior, helping reduce vibration artifacts rather than relying on assembly alone.

I also applied closed-loop control to maintain chamber temperature. Together, motion tuning and temperature regulation made this a controls and integration project as well as a fabrication exercise.
